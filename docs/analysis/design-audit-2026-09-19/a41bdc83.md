**A1 · Pass 4 — the SQS polling estimate is 2–5× low, and the "$0.00, meaningful headroom" conclusion does not hold at the top of the range.**
**Status: WRONG (verified).**

The design states ~130,000 `ReceiveMessage` per queue per month, ~260,000 total, against 1 M free.

That figure corresponds to **one** long poller. AWS documents that an SQS event source mapping starts with **five** concurrent pollers and, when idle, "scales back the processing to five concurrent invokes, and can optimize to as few as 2". At a 20-second long poll:

| Pollers | Per queue / month | Two ESMs |
|---|---|---|
| 5 (documented start) | ~657,000 | **~1,314,000** |
| 2 (optimised floor) | ~263,000 | ~526,000 |

So two enabled mappings cost roughly **0.5 M–1.3 M requests/month**, and at the documented starting point the pair **exceeds** the 1 M free tier. The design's number is below even the optimised floor.

Corrected statement: *"Two event source mappings poll continuously while enabled. At long-poll intervals with the documented 2–5 concurrent pollers, that is 0.5 M–1.3 M FIFO requests/month against a 1 M/month free tier — so the pair may sit just above the allowance. At $0.50 per million (eu-central-1 Tier 1) the exposure past the allowance is under $0.20/month."*

Keep "cents, not dollars". Drop "$0.00" and "meaningful headroom" as unconditional.

The **free tier itself is correct**: "All customers can make 1 million Amazon SQS requests for free each month", calculated across all regions, with no queue-type restriction stated. The FIFO price is also exact — see A9.

**A2 · Pass 3 — the multipart-checksum claim is outdated. Right conclusion, wrong reason.**
**Status: WRONG (verified). Requirement 15.4 mandates a false statement into the manifest.**

Design: *"A multipart upload's stored checksum is a composite of part digests, not a digest of the whole object, so it cannot be compared against a locally computed whole-file hash."*

S3 now supports **full-object** checksums on multipart uploads. CRC32, CRC32C and CRC64NVME all support `ChecksumType: FULL_OBJECT`; CRC64NVME supports *only* full-object and is the default S3 applies when you upload without a checksum. Only MD5/SHA-family digests remain composite-only.

So a whole-object comparison **is** available for a multipart upload.

The design's *conclusion* nonetheless survives, for a reason it does not give: reading the stored checksum back requires `GetObjectAttributes` or `HeadObject` with `ChecksumMode`, both in the `GetObject` permission family that criterion 40.9 forbids. Verification by existence-and-size stands because the checksum is unreadable under this IAM stance, not because it is composite.

Corrected statement: *"S3 can store a whole-object checksum for a multipart upload when a CRC algorithm and `FULL_OBJECT` type are used, but reading it back needs a GetObject-family permission the design withholds. Verification is therefore existence and size, and the manifest claims no digest."*

- [Checking object integrity for data at rest](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/checking-object-integrity-at-rest.html) — "All supported checksum algorithms support both the full object and composite checksum types, except CRC-64/NVME, which supports the full object checksum type only."
- [S3 API — Checksum](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/API/API_Checksum.html)

**A3 · Pass 3 — asset reconciliation cannot detect a partially-downloaded asset, and the `missing` bucket will always read 0.**
**Status: WRONG (verified against library source).**

I read `lib/tasks/download-assets.js` on `master`. Two mechanical consequences the design misses:

1. `createWriteStream(localFile)` is opened **before** the HTTP GET and is **never unlinked** on failure. A failed download therefore leaves a **zero-byte file**, not an absent one. So every download failure lands in the design's `truncated` bucket and `missing` stays 0. Any test asserting `missing >= 1` for a failed asset will be wrong, and the manifest's two counters do not mean what they read as.

2. A transfer that dies mid-stream leaves a **partial, non-zero** file. `st.size === 0` does not catch it. The library's own source comment makes this the expected failure mode: *"For streaming responses, axios timeout applies to time-to-first-byte, not total transfer duration."* A stalled download is exactly what produces a truncated-but-non-empty file, and the design's walk passes it as complete.

Correction, at no extra cost: compare `st.size` against `asset.fields.file[locale].details.size`, which is already in the returned data the design is iterating. Zero is the degenerate case of that comparison, not a separate check.

Related, smaller: an asset whose `file[locale]` lacks a `url` is counted as an **error** by the library, but the design's `if (!file?.url) continue` drops it from `expected` — so a real export defect becomes invisible to the shortfall count.

- [contentful-export `lib/tasks/download-assets.js`](https://github.com/contentful/contentful-export/blob/master/lib/tasks/download-assets.js)

**A6 · Pass 1 — the KMS/SNS grant is *more* certain than the design says, and `Resource: "*"` is a real over-grant that a template test will flag.**
**Status: sufficiency VERIFIED (design under-confident); shape WRONG.**

Two separate answers to your question.

*Is the ViaService form sufficient, or does the AWS-managed key policy still gate it?* It does not gate it. AWS's `kms:CallerAccount` documentation shows the literal AWS-managed-key policy shape — `Principal: {"AWS": "*"}` with `kms:CallerAccount` + `kms:ViaService`, allowing `kms:Decrypt` and `kms:GenerateDataKey*` — and states that **"All AWS managed keys use a `kms:ViaService` condition key in their key policy document."** So the managed key already admits every in-account identity whose request arrives through the owning service; the identity policy is the other half of a same-account union. `kms:ViaService` is populated because an SNS→KMS call is a forward access session ("This condition key only applies for Forward access sessions").

The SNS sentence the design leans on — *"AWS KMS requires explicitly naming the full ARN of KMSs in specific regions in the `Resource` section of an IAM policy"* — is about **aliases not being valid key identifiers**, not about `*` being invalid. The design treats it as making the grant unproven; it does not. Keep the commissioning test, but as a smoke test rather than a coin flip, and demote the three fallbacks accordingly.

*Is `Resource: "*"` the right shape?* No — it is an over-grant, and yes, a template test will flag it (cfn-nag W12, cfn-lint and IAM Access Analyzer all flag wildcard resources in IAM policies). As written it grants `GenerateDataKey`/`Decrypt` on **every** KMS key reachable through SNS in any account and region, not just `alias/aws/sns`. Two tighter forms need no lookup:

- `Resource: !Sub 'arn:${AWS::Partition}:kms:${AWS::Region}:${AWS::AccountId}:key/*'` — strictly narrower, scoped to this account and region, still nothing to resolve.
- Add `"ForAnyValue:StringEquals": { "kms:ResourceAliases": "alias/aws/sns" }`. This condition key exists **for exactly this problem**: controlling access by the key's alias when you cannot name the key. IAM policies only; fails if the key exceeds the aliases-per-key quota.

Verified in the design's favour on the same page: a customer-managed key **is** required for service-principal publishers ("Use a customer managed key"), and *"Amazon SNS reuses a data key for up to 5 minutes"* — so the 5-minute claim and the "KMS request volume is immaterial" conclusion are both sound.

- [AWS KMS condition keys — `kms:CallerAccount`, `kms:ViaService`, `kms:ResourceAliases`](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/conditions-kms.html)
- [Managing Amazon SNS encryption keys and costs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sns/latest/dg/sns-key-management.html)

**B4 · Pass 3 — the streamed upload. Verified, with two caveats worth writing down.**

`lib-storage` `Upload` streaming a multipart upload from a Node stream: **VERIFIED** — *"uploading of buffers, blobs, or streams, using a configurable amount of concurrency to perform multipart uploads **where possible**."*

- That "where possible" matters: a body below `partSize` (default 5 MB) goes out as a single `PutObject`. So "multipart" is not guaranteed, and for a small archive the checksum discussion in A2 changes shape again.
- "Peak memory independent of archive size" is right in substance but not literally independent — it is bounded by `partSize × queueSize`, ~20 MB at the defaults. State that number; it is what makes the `MemorySize` decision auditable against the 450 MB ceiling.
- "A consumed stream must be recreated per retry": correct in substance — a non-seekable Node stream cannot be rewound. **INFERRED** from SDK behaviour rather than one doc sentence.
- Worth knowing: `lib-storage` has a reported history of *not* retrying a failed part and completing the upload without it, producing a short object ([aws-sdk-js-v3 #2311](https://github.com/aws/aws-sdk-js-v3/issues/2311)). The design's **size** check catches that — an argument *for* the size comparison. But the in-invocation retry loop should wrap the whole `Upload`, not lean on the abstraction's internal part retries.

`HeadObject` requiring `s3:GetObject`, and the `ListObjectsV2`-scoped-to-key substitute needing only `s3:ListBucket`: consistent with the docs. Accepted.

### One structural observation

Every error in section A is of the same kind: a claim that was true, or true of a different version, and was carried forward without re-grounding. A2 (checksums), A4 (ExO defaults), A7 (code storage) and A1 (poller count) are all "the platform moved" or "the library moved". The design's own strongest work — B5, where it read the library's behaviour rather than its documentation — is the method that would have caught all four. The two claims that most need that treatment before implementation are **A5 (tags)** and **A3 (partial asset downloads)**, because both fail silently and both defeat the purpose of the backup rather than merely costing money.
